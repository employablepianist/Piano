# Concept - Pentascales

A “scale” is a specific collection of notes at the piano. A pentascale (or pentachord) is a five note scale. To start with, let's take all the notes of the C major scale (the white keys) and break them up into every possible five note chunk.

Here are the 7 pentascales that the white keys can be broken up into. Each pentascale is written multiple octaves. Octaves that are rare to read in are placed in parentheses. 
<br/>

## Finding the C major Pentascale

 Usually they’re played up and down, but as we’ll find there are many different ways to play scales.

A 5-note scale gets the name “pentascale”. Note that this is different from the term “pentatonic”, which we’ll look at later. 

The C major pentascale contains the notes C D E F G. In the left hand, it’s most commonly played with the fingering 5 4 3 2 1, and in the right hand 1 2 3 4 5. To find this scale, as before, find the groups of two black notes, and move to the left to find C. From C, move up playing one white note after another.

The when we play two notes that are right next to each other we call that stepwise motion.

In this diagram, left hand fingerings are in gray circles, right hand fingerings are in white circles.

## Connections to Mikrokosmos

Notice that Mikrokosmos uses 5 note chunks often. The first seven pieces use only white keys: 
- 1 and 2a both use the C major pentascale
- 2a uses A minor
- 3 uses D minor
- 4 uses B locrian (can also be thought of as a fragment of a full scale, more on that later)
- 5 uses G major (same deal here)
- 6 uses G major
- 7 uses E phrygian.
<br/>

## Position Work

Oftentimes pianists will refer to a 5-note shape as a "position". In my writings I will refer to these shapes as such as well.

To clarify, this doesn't mean the hand is *frozen* in a five note position, with each finger touching a distinct key. Rather, it just implies a small sense of "gravity" towards a set of 5 notes - a set of notes wherein we won't have to move the hand/arm too much in order to move from note to note. However, we'll still have to move the hand/arm a little bit!

5-note positions also have specific in-out choreographies and shaping choreographies. We'll get to this later.
<br/>

## Resolving to a Note In the Middle of a Position

Sometimes notes create tensions that want to pull to other notes. When tense notes want to move we say they're *resolving*. 

So far we have looked at Pentascales which *resolve* to their lowest note. However, music doesn't *always* work this way, and just because we're in a 5-note position doesn't mean we have to resolve to the thumb as home-base.

Look at the scale that precedes *Mikrokosmos 4*. B is the bottom note, but the scale resolves to C. This is technically a B locrian pentascale, but what's really happening is that the 5-note position is a fragment of the *full C major scale*.
